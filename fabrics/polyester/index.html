Fabric

Heat pressing polyester

Polyester does not have one heat press temperature. Everyday HTV, DTF and sublimation are published at different heats, and this is the fabric most likely to shine or bleed dye.

These are starting points from the charts named below. Read the instructions for the product you are pressing, then test on a scrap or a spare blank. The number on a heat press dial is often not the true platen temperature.

Quick answer

Sawgrass print both 400°F and 205°C. Siser’s EasyWeed page and the Xpres guides print Celsius only. Siser print the EasyWeed pressure as 3/4 bar. This site reads that as about 3–4 bar, the same way it reads their 4/5 bar as about 4–5 bar. The usual conversion is 302°F for 150°C, 302–320°F for 150–160°C, and 374°F for 190°C. A straight conversion of 205°C is 401°F, so use the 400°F Sawgrass print beside 205°C.

Use the row that matches the transfer in your hand. A film box or a blank card that prints its own figures replaces the row. Percentage, white fabric and a ghost image on sublimation paper are on the sublimation on polyester page.

Heat transfer vinyl

Siser’s P.S. Film EasyWeed page lists 100% polyester with 100% cotton and poly/cotton blends, all at 150°C for 15 seconds, medium pressure, carrier off hot or cold. The blend line adds “not for dye sublimated fabrics.” Other films publish other heats. Match the roll on the standard HTV page, and keep a hotter film on the heat that film publishes.

Scorch and shiny marks

A shiny rectangle is the polyester. Siser’s heat-sensitive article names 100% polyester, and also rayon, silk and anything you are unsure about. They say discolouration is sometimes temporary and sometimes permanent, and they tell you to test press a hidden area, such as the inside of the back when the care label says cool iron or do not iron. Their cover is a heat-transfer cover sheet, or parchment, or the shiny side of multipurpose paper.

The lower temperature they publish for this problem is HI-5. The article gives 120°C for 5 seconds, and says that avoids shine and fibre damage on the most common polyester-based fabric. The HI-5 product page splits it by weight: textiles under 150 g/m² at 120°C, heavier textiles at 150°C, both for 5 seconds at high pressure (4/5 bar on the page, about 4–5 bar), carrier off hot or cold.

When the film is something else, Siser say you can lower the temperature and raise the time. The only worked example on that page is StripFlock Pro: normally 15 seconds at 155°C, or 25–30 seconds and not below 140°C. That alternative belongs to StripFlock Pro. The scorch marks page has the same notes.

Dye migration

Light vinyl on a dark or sublimated polyester can shift colour after the press. Siser’s dye-migration note says the dye in the shirt moves when it is heated. They see it most on sublimated polyester, and also on ordinary polyester. High temperature and high pressure are a major cause. The shift shows on light film. Siser say it only shows when light-coloured vinyl goes on a darker polyester.

The current Siser product page for P.S. Subli LT (EasyWeed Sub Block) is the blocker chart: 135°C for 15 seconds, medium pressure (3/4 bar), carrier off hot or cold, for dye-sublimated polyester. Their North America note still prints 265°F / 130°C at medium pressure, and that page does not give a time. Follow the sheet in the box when the two disagree. Everyday vinyl settings stay on the standard HTV page.

DTF

Xpres describe DTF for cotton, poly and blends, and they do not print a separate polyester temperature. Their how-to presses at 150–160°C for 10–15 seconds, medium pressure, peel cold, then a second press under a silicone sheet. They say heavier cotton may need a slightly higher temperature, and they do not give that new number. Their information sheet, which also does not split fabrics, is 160°C for 15 seconds, light to medium, cold peel off the platen, then another 15 seconds. Use the sheet that came with the film. A shiny halo around the design is the scorch described above. Cover the shirt. The full steps are on the DTF page.

Sublimation

Light polyester is the fabric this process is for. The two published pairs are in the table: Sawgrass polyester fabric at 205°C (400°F) for 45 seconds, medium, and Xpres polyester T-shirts at 190°C for 60–70 seconds, medium, peel hot. Dark polyester has no white ink to cover it. How much polyester the shirt needs, why the fabric should be light, and how to stop a ghost on the paper are on the sublimation on polyester page.

Common questions

What temperature do you heat press polyester at?

It depends on the transfer. Siser EasyWeed, which lists 100% polyester, is 150°C for 15 seconds at medium pressure. Xpres press DTF, which they describe for cotton, poly and blends, at 150–160°C for 10–15 seconds. Sublimation is hotter: Sawgrass list polyester fabric at 205°C (400°F) for 45 seconds, and Xpres press polyester T-shirts at 190°C for 60–70 seconds.

Why is there a shiny box around the design?

The polyester has glazed. Siser’s heat-sensitive advice is a cover sheet, a test press on a hidden area, such as inside the back, and HI-5 at 120°C for 5 seconds on textiles under 150 g/m², or 150°C for 5 seconds when the textile is heavier. Their article says the shine is sometimes temporary and sometimes permanent.

Why did the white vinyl change colour?

That is dye migration. Siser say high temperature and high pressure make dye in polyester move into the film. Their Sub Block product page is 135°C for 15 seconds at medium pressure. A North America Siser note prints 265°F / 130°C at medium pressure and does not state a time.
